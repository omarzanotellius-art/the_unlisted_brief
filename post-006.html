Whose word should the market take?

The data on a power plant mostly exists. What a tokenized plant lacks is someone who stands behind its value.

In my last post on tokenized real estate, I ended on a simple idea: information about a plant would need someone to stand behind it. That idea sits at the heart of dynamic asset revaluation for tokenized power plants: a value that moves when the plant does. This post looks at what that would take. The short answer: a plant’s value can only move with its facts if those facts reach holders, someone accountable stands behind the judgment, and someone is obliged to share both on time. A single tokenized plant rarely has all three. If you negotiate your own information rights, you probably have most of this already. Most token holders don’t.

Why is a plant’s value hard to pin down?

There’s no single way to value a tokenized power plant, which makes valuation a point of tension among the different players. Whether you determine the asset’s value through a cost, income or market approach, each step involves judgment, and in my experience, reasonable people land on different numbers. That matters because every transaction relies on a valuation, whether it’s stated or simply assumed. As I see it, tokenized assets need a valuation that stays current, and the number travels into reporting and portfolios. A valuation only keeps up with the facts that reach it.

Who sees a plant’s facts, and when?

The challenge begins with information itself. Accounting standards like US GAAP and IFRS provide common ground, and their numbers are trusted. But they don’t all measure the same thing: the plant itself is usually carried at what it cost, less depreciation and amortization, while fair value (ASC 820, IFRS 13) is what the asset would sell for in a normal sale today. Either way, the numbers are periodic: a major event can trigger an impairment review, but the result appears only in the next financial statements.

The token’s value also depends on the capital structure around the plant: projects are typically owned through an SPV, with lenders and often tax equity investors holding claims that rank ahead of, or alongside, the token holder’s, so a refinancing, for example, can change the token’s economics without any change in the plant itself.

Market data runs on a different clock. Prices are public and fast: in Texas, ERCOT publishes prices for specific locations on the grid, normally every five minutes, and similar data is available across other US power markets. These prices are published through public data connections (APIs) that software can read.

Performance data is different. The plant’s monitoring system (SCADA) shows its output in real time, and if the plant sells into a competitive wholesale market, the grid operator also keeps an official record of what it produced, which the owner can access through a secure portal. The public sees only totals by region and fuel, and the EIA’s plant-level statistics arrive about two months later. Fair enough for an owner. For a token, and the infrastructure around it, what’s needed is a single source of truth for the plant’s facts that everyone holding a stake can see and trust.

A token can record who owns a plant. It can’t, on its own, keep the plant’s value honest.

Borrowed trust, earned trust

The owner already has most of the facts, and has the least reason to publish bad ones quickly. What I think the market seeks is not just more information, but a source of judgment it can trust. When a value affects lending decisions, investor reporting, or secondary-market transactions, and reasonable valuers can disagree, a buyer or a lender wants to know who is prepared to defend that assessment if challenged and who bears responsibility if it proves wrong. That is the role independent engineers, auditors, and valuers have historically played. Their value isn’t in producing data; it’s in standing behind a conclusion. Project-finance lenders to the same plant typically already get this: regular operating reports, covenant tests and an independent engineer’s sign-off.

Tokenized funds already show part of this. Apollo’s tokenized private-credit fund is priced daily, and that value is made available on-chain, where other systems can read it. No rating agency certifies the number. It’s accepted because of what surrounds it: a fund administrator, auditors, a documented valuation process, and a manager with a reputation and legal exposure if it gets the number wrong. The judgment isn’t new. What’s new is that it arrives every day, in a form software can use.

A single power plant, tokenized on its own, isn’t there yet. Renewable projects may already sit inside large tokenized funds, but RWA.xyz, one of the main trackers of tokenized assets, doesn’t even give infrastructure a category of its own. Many renewable assets are also small, scattered across sponsors few investors have heard of. Their value can’t borrow trust from a name; it would have to earn it from facts others can check, at a cost a small plant can carry.

Where single physical assets are tokenized, the value tends to stand still: as I noted in my last post, many real estate tokens still show the value they had on the day they were issued. A plant’s conclusions usually arrive in a periodic report, long after the facts have changed. For a tokenized plant to earn that trust, the judgment behind its value has to be shared on time, on the same digital rails as its data, not in a PDF that arrives when it suits the owner.

Dynamic asset revaluation, piece by piece

The way I read it, three pieces are missing. The first is a shared record of the plant’s facts that everyone holding a stake can see. The second is someone accountable for the judgment built on those facts. The third is an obligation to share both on time, which digital rails make far easier to meet. For a token that trades, the first piece may matter most: the market can price facts it can see.

Without them, events reach a token late. Among the events I’ve witnessed on solar farms, two show the difference: one temporary, one that changed the asset itself. In one, a transformer fire took the plant offline for two months; insurance covered part of the loss, and the plant still lost value. In another, a hailstorm forced modules to be replaced ahead of schedule; insurance paid, but the deductible and the early spending still cut into value. Output data shows events like these the same day, while the assessments, insurance claims and books take longer. If holders could see an attested, delayed version of that data, the market would already know something had happened, even before anyone could say what it means for value. Visible data changes the owner’s incentive: the larger the drop, the harder it is to stay silent. How long should the token’s value have to wait to reflect it?

What a moving value can’t fix

If a token can be redeemed at its published value, its price stays close to it. If it only trades, the market sets the price, and the published value is one reference among others. Either way, a valuation is an opinion, even when software produces it. Even the facts can be disputed, such as whether an outage counts against availability or a curtailment is paid for. And a number that changes every day isn’t better informed than a quarterly one if the view behind it hasn’t changed.

Once a value feeds decisions that move money, the incentive to influence it grows. In a thin market, news without an assessment can do harm of its own: some holders may sell before anyone knows how much the value has changed. Most new information is noise. The challenge is neither to update value continuously nor to leave it unchanged too long. It’s knowing when new information matters enough to justify a fresh look. That’s where judgment comes in, and where shared standards, and a trustworthy party behind them, paid in a way that doesn’t depend on the answer, become valuable.

When a plant’s value changes, whose word should the market take?
